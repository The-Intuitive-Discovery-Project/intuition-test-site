Yes or No

Three simple tools. One important skill: learning how to ask a clear question and get a clear answer. In this class you will learn a simple yes/no method using ordinary playing cards, a pendulum, and dowsing rods. You will also learn how to make your own tools for little or no cost, how to practice with answers you can verify, and why the wording of your question can completely change what you think an answer means.

Important: This class is for spiritual exploration, intuition practice, reflection, and education.

Do not use these methods instead of medical, mental-health, legal, emergency, safety, or other qualified professional advice.

What you are learning

This is deliberately focused. We are not learning full cartomancy readings. We are learning how to work with a binary question: yes or no.

1. Before the tools: what are you actually doing?

A yes/no practice takes something complicated and temporarily reduces it to two possible answers. That simplicity can be useful, but it can also create false confidence if the question itself is vague.

Playing cardsYou establish a simple rule before drawing. In this class: red = yes, black = no.
PendulumYou establish which movement represents yes, no, and ideally unclear/not now.
Dowsing rodsYou establish your yes/no movements before asking the real question.
Your responsibilityYou ask clearly, stay as neutral as possible, record the answer, and validate when reality gives you feedback.
Hunter's approach: Guides guide; they do not decide. These tools are part of your spiritual and intuitive practice, but you remain responsible for your choices.

2. The art of the yes/no question

This may be the most valuable part of the entire class. A tool can give a perfectly clear response to a badly written question—and the answer can still be almost useless.

Rule 1 — Define words that can mean different things

Suppose you ask:

“Will my boyfriend and I get back together soon?”

What does soon mean? Tomorrow? Two weeks? Two months? Your idea of soon may not be the same as somebody else's, and it is not a measurable time frame.

Try instead:

“Will my boyfriend and I be back in a committed relationship within the next 30 days?”

Now you have defined both the outcome and the time window.

Rule 2 — A “no” may only be a no to the time frame

This is one of the easiest mistakes to make. Ask, “Will we get back together within one month?” and receive no. That does not automatically mean “we will never get back together.” It only means the answer to the question you actually asked was no.

One month: No. Two months: possibly Yes.
The first answer was not necessarily wrong. You changed the question.

This is why you should write down the exact wording instead of remembering a shortened version of it later.

Rule 3 — Ask one thing at a time

Avoid compound questions.

Too much: “Will I get the job and be happy there?”

Better: “Will I be offered this specific job by October 1?” Then, separately, explore the second issue.

One part of a compound question could be yes while another is no. A binary tool has no clean way to explain that.

Rule 4 — Make it measurable when possible

Words such as soon, successful, better, rich, happy, good, enough can be slippery. Define what you mean.

Vague: “Will my business do better?”
Clearer: “Will my business receive at least 10 paid orders during October?”
Rule 5 — Keep the wording neutral

If you desperately want one answer, it becomes harder to separate intuition from hope, fear, and expectation.

Instead of repeatedly asking until you get the answer you want, ask once, record it, and let reality eventually validate it.

Rule 6 — Do not turn a yes/no tool into a guarantee

A yes/no response is guidance or practice—not permission to ignore important real-world information. For medical, mental-health, legal, emergency, or safety matters, use the appropriate qualified professional or reliable source.

Quick question checklist
  • Can this genuinely be answered yes or no?
  • Am I asking only one thing?
  • Have I defined vague words?
  • Does a time frame matter?
  • Will I know what a yes means?
  • Will I know what a no means?
  • Am I calm enough to accept either answer?
  • Can the result eventually be verified?

3. Prepare before asking

You do not need an elaborate ritual. The goal is to reduce distraction and expectation.

  1. Put the phone down or silence notifications.
  2. Take a few natural breaths.
  3. Ground yourself in whatever way works for you: feel your feet, notice the chair, listen to the room, or use a spiritual grounding practice if that fits your beliefs.
  4. Write the question exactly as you will ask it.
  5. Read it once. Check for vague words and double questions.
  6. Decide the tool's yes/no rules before receiving an answer.
  7. Ask once.
  8. Record the answer before interpreting or changing anything.
ADHD-friendly: Keep a tiny session note: Date → Exact Question → Tool → Answer → Later Result. You do not need a long journal.

4. Tool One: ordinary playing cards

A standard 52-card deck is inexpensive, common, portable, and perfect for a very simple binary practice. You do not need Tarot knowledge and you do not need to learn the meanings of the cards for this method.

HEARTS ♥ DIAMONDS ♦ = YES

CLUBS ♣ SPADES ♠ = NO
The one-card method
  1. Remove jokers. Use the normal 52 cards.
  2. Write and say your yes/no question.
  3. Shuffle while keeping the question in mind. You do not need a special shuffle.
  4. Stop when you feel drawn to.
  5. Place the top card down.
  6. Red card: yes. Black card: no.
  7. Write the result down. Stop there.

For this class, resist the urge to turn the card into a reading. A Queen of Hearts is not a relationship message here. It is red, so it is yes. A Two of Spades is not a story about conflict. It is black, so it is no. Keeping the method simple is the point.

Practice without predicting anything

Use facts you already know, but make them genuinely binary.

“Is my first name Hunter?”
“Am I currently inside a building?”
“Is today [correct day]?”
Then deliberately ask a few questions whose answer is no.

Known-answer practice teaches the routine without the emotional pressure of a future question.

Common card mistakes
  • Changing red/black meanings after seeing the card.
  • Drawing again because you do not like the answer.
  • Adding card meanings when the exercise is only yes/no.
  • Asking the same question over and over.
  • Using a vague question and blaming the deck.

5. Tool Two: the pendulum

A pendulum is simply a freely hanging weight suspended from a string, cord, or chain. You can buy a beautiful crystal or metal pendulum if you want one. You do not need one to begin.

Make a free or inexpensive pendulum

You need two things: something that hangs and something for it to hang from.

Possible weightsRing, washer, nut, key, pendant, bead, small stone securely wrapped or attached.
Possible suspensionString, thread doubled for strength, thin cord, necklace chain.
  1. Choose a small object with enough weight to hang steadily.
  2. Attach it securely to roughly 6–10 inches of string or chain. Exact length is not sacred; adjust until it moves freely and feels controllable.
  3. Make sure the object cannot fall off.
  4. Hold the free end between thumb and finger and let the weight hang.

A purchased pendulum can feel nicer in the hand and may swing more evenly, but a simple homemade pendulum works perfectly well for learning.

Calibrate: let your pendulum show you its language

Do not assume clockwise always means yes or side-to-side always means no. Establish your own signals.

  1. Rest your elbow or forearm comfortably if that helps reduce strain.
  2. Let the pendulum become still.
  3. Say, “Show me yes.” Observe the movement.
  4. Still it again.
  5. Say, “Show me no.” Observe the different movement.
  6. Then ask, “Show me unclear / I don't know / not now.” A third response is useful because not every question deserves a forced yes or no.
  7. Repeat the calibration and make sure the movements remain distinguishable.

Your yes might be forward/back, side-to-side, clockwise, or something else. The consistency matters more than copying somebody else's directions.

Known-answer calibration

After “show me yes/no,” test the signals with facts.

  1. Ask your real name. It should produce your yes signal.
  2. Ask a deliberately false name. It should produce no.
  3. Ask several other easy, factual questions.
  4. If responses are inconsistent, stop. Reset, ground, and practice later rather than forcing it.
Ask a real yes/no question
  1. Write the exact question.
  2. Calibrate yes/no/unclear.
  3. Still the pendulum.
  4. Ask once in a neutral voice.
  5. Allow the pendulum to respond without trying to control the answer.
  6. Observe which established signal develops.
  7. Record it immediately.
When the pendulum will not answer clearly

Do not force a messy movement into yes or no. Check:

  • Is the question actually binary?
  • Did you ask two things at once?
  • Did you use an undefined word such as “soon”?
  • Are you physically tired or shaky?
  • Are you highly emotional about the answer?
  • Did you establish an unclear/not-now signal?

Sometimes the best answer for the session is simply: I do not have a clear answer.

6. Tool Three: dowsing rods

For yes/no work, L-rods are easy to understand because the movement is large and visible. As with a pendulum, you can buy balanced rods with handles—or make a simple pair yourself.

Make your own L-rods

A common DIY version uses two pieces of reasonably stiff wire. An old wire coat hanger can work if it can be safely cut and straightened.

Safety: Cut wire can have sharp ends. Use appropriate wire cutters, eye protection, and adult help where needed. Smooth, cap, or tape sharp ends. Never use the rods around exposed electrical equipment or anywhere their length could create a hazard.
  1. Make two similar pieces of wire.
  2. Bend each into an L shape: a shorter section becomes the handle and a longer section points forward.
  3. A rough starting point is a 4–6 inch handle and a 10–16 inch forward arm. They do not have to be exact twins.
  4. For free-spinning handles, slide the short ends into empty pen barrels, short straws, or small tubes. The wire should rotate without binding.
  5. Hold one rod in each hand with the long arms roughly level and pointing forward.

You can absolutely purchase professionally made rods later. Buying them is convenience, not a requirement for learning.

How to hold them
  1. Stand or sit comfortably.
  2. Keep elbows near your sides without locking your arms.
  3. Hold the handles gently so the rods can move freely.
  4. Point the long arms forward and roughly parallel.
  5. Give the rods room to swing without hitting your body or nearby objects.
Establish yes, no, and unclear
  1. Start with rods forward.
  2. Ask, “Show me yes.” Let the rods move and note the pattern.
  3. Reset them to neutral.
  4. Ask, “Show me no.” Note the different pattern.
  5. Ask for “unclear / not now.”
  6. Repeat until you can recognize your established responses.

Some people's rods cross for yes and open for no. Others establish something different. Do not borrow somebody else's answer system after the fact. Calibrate first.

Known-answer practice with rods

Use the same type of questions you used with the pendulum. Start with several obvious yes answers and several obvious no answers. Reset the rods between questions.

If the rods repeatedly fail known-answer tests, that is useful feedback. Work on your grip, neutrality, question wording, and practice rather than declaring every movement meaningful.

7. When a question matters: use all three while learning

You have three different ways to practice. Until you have built confidence and a record of validation, I recommend checking an important practice-level question with all three.

Question: Write it once. Do not change the wording between tools.

Playing cards: ______
Pendulum: ______
Dowsing rods: ______

Agreement: 3/3, 2/3, or no clear agreement?
What actually happened later? ______
If two say yes and one says no

Do not hide the disagreement. Record all three. You can note the majority response, but the disagreement is part of your practice notes.

If everything disagrees

That is not failure. Check the question, your emotional state, and your calibration. Put it down and return later if appropriate. “Unclear” is more honest than forcing a conclusion.

Do not keep asking until you get 3 yeses

If you repeat the same question again and again, eventually you may get the combination you want. That teaches you nothing. One clean attempt per tool is much more useful for validation.

8. Intuition vs. fear, hope, imagination, and mental noise

One of the hardest skills is not operating the tool. It is noticing what you bring to the tool.

Hope“I need this to say yes.”
Fear“I already know something bad is going to happen.”
Mental noiseRapid arguments, possibilities, and what-ifs.
ImaginationA thought or scenario your mind creates. Imagination is not bad; it simply should not automatically be labeled intuition.
Intuitive impressionOften described as a quieter first knowing, feeling, body response, word, or impression—but the way to learn your own pattern is validation, not a definition.
ValidationRecord first. Find out later. Compare. That is how you learn what was useful.
Notice → Name → Validate → Reflect. Notice what happened. Name it without exaggerating. Validate when possible. Reflect on what your accurate and inaccurate attempts felt like.

9. Build skill with validation

Give yourself time to practice and learn what a clear intuitive response feels like for you.

Stage 1 — Known answers

Do 10–20 simple questions where you know the truth. Mix yes and no. The goal is learning the mechanics.

Stage 2 — Hidden but immediately verifiable

Have somebody prepare simple targets you do not know, or use randomized factual practice where the answer can be revealed immediately.

Stage 3 — Short-term questions

Use low-stakes questions with a clear outcome and short time window. Record them before the outcome occurs.

Stage 4 — Compare the three tools

Track each tool separately. You may discover that one feels easier or produces more consistent validation for you. You do not need to be equally comfortable with all three.

Stage 5 — Let your record teach you

After enough practice, review:

  • Which tool feels most natural?
  • Which tool validates most often?
  • Do emotional questions lower your consistency?
  • Do vague questions cause more confusion?
  • What does a genuinely clear response feel like for you?

11. Troubleshooting

“My pendulum barely moves.”

Check that the weight hangs freely, shorten or lengthen the cord, support your arm comfortably, and practice with known answers. A huge swing is not required.

“My rods move before I finish asking.”

Reset, relax your grip, and write the question first. Give yourself a moment to settle before asking.

“I keep getting the answer I want.”

Switch to verifiable neutral practice for a while. If you cannot accept no, it may not be a good time to ask that question.

“I asked again and the answer changed.”

First ask whether anything in the question actually changed: wording, time frame, facts, or your method. If nothing changed, repeated questioning may simply be adding noise. Use the first properly calibrated attempt in your practice record.

“Which tool is the best?”

The best practice tool is the one you can use consistently and validate. Cards are extremely accessible. Pendulums are compact. Rods provide large visible movement. Try all three before deciding.

12. Ethics and boundaries

  • Do not diagnose illness or decide treatment with these tools.
  • Do not replace legal, safety, emergency, or other qualified professional information with a yes/no response.
  • Be careful about turning another person's private life into endless questions.
  • Do not use repeated yes/no checking to feed anxiety or compulsive reassurance-seeking. Put the tools away if the practice is making you more distressed.
  • Do not promise certainty to yourself or somebody else.
  • Remember that circumstances and human choices can change.
  • Use the tools to support reflection—not surrender your judgment.

Your complete session

  1. Ground. Get present.
  2. Write. One clear, neutral, time-aware question.
  3. Check. Remove vague words and double questions.
  4. Calibrate. Establish the tool's responses before the real question.
  5. Ask once. Do not chase an answer.
  6. Record. Exact question + exact result.
  7. Cross-check while learning. Use the same wording with cards, pendulum, and rods when appropriate.
  8. Validate. Let the real outcome tell you what happened.
  9. Reflect. Learn your own patterns.

You are not trying to become perfect. You are trying to become consistent, clear, and honest with yourself.

Final thought

Anyone can pick up a deck of cards, hang a ring from a string, or hold two pieces of bent wire. The deeper practice is learning patience, good questions, neutrality, and validation. A clear tool with a vague question can still confuse you. A beautiful expensive pendulum does not make the question better. Start with what you have. Practice on things you can verify. Keep the exact wording. Learn what your own accurate and inaccurate attempts feel like. And remember: guidance can help you reflect, but you are still the person living your life and making the decision.

Meditation: Start Where You Are

Meditation is not about completely clearing your mind. It is about giving yourself a moment to slow down, ground yourself, step out of your routine, and notice what is happening inside you. You do not have to do it perfectly. Your mind can wander. You can move. You can open your eyes. The more you practice, the more familiar it becomes. There is no single “right” way to meditate. If you are here and willing to try, you have already taken the biggest step: you started.

Health & safety: This class is for education, relaxation, reflection, and personal development. Meditation is not a substitute for medical or mental-health care and should not be used to delay professional treatment. Meditation can occasionally bring up anxiety, distressing thoughts, memories, or uncomfortable feelings. If a practice feels overwhelming, stop, open your eyes, orient yourself to your surroundings, move around, and choose something that feels safer. Seek qualified professional help when you need it. Never meditate while driving or doing anything that requires your full attention.

This is a menu, not homework. Learn the basics, then pick one practice that fits the time and energy you have today.

1. What meditation actually is

Different meditations use different anchors: your breath, physical sensations, sounds, movement, words, or simple present-moment awareness. The skill is not “having no thoughts.” The practice is noticing where your attention went and gently returning.

ADHD-friendly rule: A wandering mind does not mean you failed. Noticing that your mind wandered is part of the meditation.
Eyes closed or openUse whichever helps you feel comfortable and present.
Sitting, lying, standing, or walkingYour body does not need a perfect meditation pose.
Guided or silentSome people focus better with a voice; others prefer quiet.
Imagery optionalIf you do not visualize easily, use words, sensations, sounds, or simple knowing instead.

2. A simple way to begin any meditation

Notice → Settle → Anchor → Return → Finish
  1. Notice: Check in with your body and mind without judging what you find.
  2. Settle: Get reasonably comfortable. You do not have to become perfectly still.
  3. Anchor: Pick one thing to return to—breath, sound, body sensation, footsteps, or a phrase.
  4. Return: When thoughts pull you away, notice it and come back. Repeat as many times as needed.
  5. Finish: Take a moment before jumping back into your day. Notice whether anything shifted.

3. Short meditations: 5–10 minutes

These are for busy days, beginners, restless days, or anytime a longer session feels like too much.

1. Five-Minute Breath Anchor
5 MINUTES

Use the natural breath as a home base without forcing it to become slower or deeper.

  1. Get comfortable and notice where you feel breathing most clearly.
  2. Follow one inhale from beginning to end, then one exhale.
  3. If counting helps, count each exhale from one to five and start again.
  4. When thoughts appear, silently note “thinking” and return to the next breath.
  5. Finish by noticing your whole body for a few breaths.
2. Five Senses Grounding Meditation
5–8 MINUTES

A good eyes-open option when focusing inward feels difficult.

  1. Notice five things you can see.
  2. Notice four physical sensations: feet, clothing, chair, temperature.
  3. Notice three sounds.
  4. Notice two scents, even if they are faint.
  5. Notice one taste or simply the feeling inside your mouth.
  6. Take one natural breath and notice where you are right now.
3. Sound Meditation
5–10 MINUTES

Let sound be the anchor instead of trying to block it out.

  1. Listen first for the closest sound.
  2. Then notice sounds farther away.
  3. Notice loud, quiet, steady, and changing sounds without naming every source.
  4. If your attention wanders, return to listening.
  5. For the last minute, hear the whole soundscape at once.
4. Mini Body Scan
7–10 MINUTES

Move attention through the body without needing to change what you feel.

  1. Start at your feet and notice pressure, temperature, tingling, or nothing at all.
  2. Move through legs, hips, stomach, chest, hands, arms, shoulders, neck, and face.
  3. Pause briefly at each area.
  4. If you find tension, allow it to be there; soften only if that happens naturally.
  5. Finish by sensing the body as one whole.
5. One-Word / Mantra Meditation
5–10 MINUTES

Choose a simple word or phrase such as “calm,” “here,” “I am present,” or another phrase that feels genuine.

  1. Say the word silently at an easy rhythm.
  2. Let the word be your anchor.
  3. When thoughts interrupt, return to the phrase without arguing with the thought.
  4. Leave a little silence between repetitions if that feels comfortable.
6. Loving-Kindness Practice
8–10 MINUTES

Practice directing goodwill toward yourself and others without forcing an emotion.

  1. Begin with yourself: “May I be safe. May I have peace. May I meet today with kindness.”
  2. Bring to mind someone you care about and offer similar wishes.
  3. If you want, include someone neutral.
  4. Finish by extending the wish outward more broadly.

You do not have to visualize anyone. A name, memory, or simple sense of the person is enough.

7. Morning Reset
5–8 MINUTES

A short practice before the day gets noisy.

  1. Notice three breaths.
  2. Notice how your body feels this morning.
  3. Ask: “What do I actually need today?”
  4. Choose one quality to bring into the day—patience, courage, focus, kindness, or something else.
  5. Sit quietly with that intention for a few minutes.
8. Evening Release
8–10 MINUTES

A transition practice for putting down the day without pretending everything is resolved.

  1. Feel the support beneath your body.
  2. Notice three things from today that are still occupying your mind.
  3. For each one, say silently: “I can return to this later. I do not have to solve it right now.”
  4. Return to the breath or body.
  5. End by noticing one thing you are ready to leave with today.

4. Medium-length practices: 12–25 minutes

9. Breath → Body → Sound
12–15 MINUTES

Changing anchors can help when staying on one thing feels too repetitive.

  1. Spend a few minutes following the breath.
  2. Widen attention to physical sensations throughout the body.
  3. Then let sounds become the main focus.
  4. Finally allow breath, body, and sound to share awareness.
10. Progressive Muscle Awareness
15–20 MINUTES

Notice the contrast between tension and release. Keep movements gentle and skip any area that hurts.

  1. Beginning with feet, lightly tense one area for a few seconds.
  2. Release and notice the difference.
  3. Move gradually upward through legs, hands, arms, shoulders, and face.
  4. Finish by resting without deliberately changing anything.
11. Walking Meditation
15–20 MINUTES

Meditation does not require sitting still.

  1. Choose a safe place where you do not need to watch traffic.
  2. Walk at a natural or slightly slower pace.
  3. Feel each foot lift, move, and meet the ground.
  4. Notice body movement, sounds, temperature, and air.
  5. Each time you get lost in thought, return to the next step.
12. Working With a Difficult Feeling
15–20 MINUTES

This is about making room for a manageable feeling, not forcing yourself into overwhelming material.

  1. Ground first with your feet, breath, or sounds around you.
  2. Name what is present simply: “worry,” “sadness,” “frustration,” or “uncertainty.”
  3. Notice where you feel it physically, if anywhere.
  4. Alternate attention between that sensation and a neutral anchor.
  5. Remind yourself: “I can feel this without solving it this second.”
  6. If distress increases, stop and orient outward.
13. Self-Compassion Meditation
15–20 MINUTES

Practice responding to yourself the way you might respond to someone you genuinely care about.

  1. Notice something that has been difficult without choosing your most overwhelming experience.
  2. Acknowledge: “This is hard right now.”
  3. Remember that struggling is part of being human.
  4. Ask what kind of response would actually be supportive.
  5. Rest with a phrase such as “May I give myself some patience here.”
14. Intuition Listening Meditation
15–25 MINUTES

An optional spiritual/personal-reflection practice. It is not a prediction exercise and you do not need to receive an answer.

  1. Ground with breath and body sensations.
  2. Let mental chatter settle as much as it naturally does.
  3. Ask one simple open question, such as “What deserves my attention today?”
  4. Stop asking and simply notice thoughts, feelings, body signals, words, or impressions.
  5. Do not force meaning. Write down what you noticed afterward and reflect later.

5. Longer meditations: 30–40 minutes

Longer does not automatically mean better. These sessions simply give you more room to settle and explore.

15. Full Body Scan
30–40 MINUTES

A slow tour through the body, noticing rather than fixing.

  1. Spend several minutes settling with the breath.
  2. Move attention slowly from toes through each region of the body.
  3. Notice pressure, warmth, coolness, pulsing, tension, ease, numbness, or no obvious sensation.
  4. When attention wanders, return to the body area you were exploring.
  5. End with several minutes sensing the entire body breathing and resting.
16. Deep Grounding Meditation
30–35 MINUTES

Build stability through physical awareness first; optional spiritual imagery comes second.

  1. Spend 5 minutes noticing contact with the floor or chair.
  2. Spend 10 minutes slowly scanning the body.
  3. Spend 10 minutes following breath and sounds while feeling physically supported.
  4. Optional: imagine, sense, or simply think of yourself as connected securely to the earth. Visualization is not required.
  5. Finish by moving hands and feet and looking around the room.
17. Open Awareness Meditation
30–40 MINUTES

Instead of holding one anchor the entire time, practice noticing whatever naturally becomes most prominent.

  1. Begin with 5–10 minutes of breath awareness.
  2. Gradually widen attention.
  3. Notice sounds, sensations, emotions, and thoughts as events that appear and change.
  4. Try not to chase or push away any one experience.
  5. If you become lost, return to breathing until you feel steady, then widen again.
18. Loving-Kindness Deep Practice
30–40 MINUTES

Give each stage more time: yourself, someone you love, a neutral person, someone difficult if appropriate, and finally a wider circle of people.

Important: Skip anyone who feels too emotionally activating. This is a practice in goodwill, not an obligation to excuse harmful behavior.

6. Open-ended meditation: stay as long as you want

19. Silent Sit With a Chosen Anchor
NO SET LENGTH

Pick breath, body, sound, or a word. Stay with it for as little or as long as feels useful. You can decide beforehand that you will stop when your body tells you it is time, or use a gentle timer if you prefer structure.

The entire practice is: notice → wander → notice → return. There is nothing else you have to accomplish.

20. Sit in the Power / Spiritual Presence
NO SET LENGTH

This optional spiritual meditation is about being present rather than trying to make something happen.

  1. Ground yourself physically first.
  2. Settle into quiet awareness.
  3. If it fits your beliefs, acknowledge Spirit, the Divine, your higher self, or simply the deeper part of yourself.
  4. Do not demand a message or sign. Let yourself sit in the experience.
  5. Notice anything that arises without deciding immediately what it means.
  6. Finish by grounding again and returning attention fully to your surroundings.

7. Which meditation should I choose?

My brain is racingTry sound, walking, five senses, or Breath → Body → Sound.
I feel disconnected from my bodyTry a mini/full body scan or grounding.
I cannot sit stillWalking meditation counts. Movement is allowed.
I want something spiritualTry Intuition Listening or Sit in the Power after grounding.
I am short on timeFive minutes is enough to practice.
I don't visualizeChoose sensation, breath, sound, words, movement, or simple intention.

8. Common questions

What if I keep thinking?

You will. The goal is not to stop thought. Each time you realize you have drifted, you have a chance to return. That return is part of the practice.

What if I get bored?

Change anchors, shorten the session, meditate while walking, or simply notice what “bored” feels like. You do not earn extra points for forcing a practice that is not working for you.

Do I need to meditate every day?

No. Consistency can make a practice more familiar, but meditation is not a pass/fail streak. Start with a schedule you can realistically return to.

How long should a beginner meditate?

Start small. Five minutes is a real meditation. Increase the time when you want more—not because you think a shorter practice “doesn't count.”

What if meditation makes me feel worse?

Stop the practice. Open your eyes, move, orient to your surroundings, and use an outward grounding activity. Meditation is not something you need to push through. If distress persists or meditation repeatedly brings up difficult symptoms, talk with a qualified health professional.

7-DAY BEGINNER PATH

If you want a little structure

  1. Day 1: Five-Minute Breath Anchor
  2. Day 2: Five Senses Grounding
  3. Day 3: Sound Meditation
  4. Day 4: Mini Body Scan
  5. Day 5: Walking Meditation
  6. Day 6: Loving-Kindness
  7. Day 7: Pick whichever one you actually want to repeat.

Remember: the goal is not to become “good at meditation” in seven days. The goal is to discover what helps you become present.

Your practice belongs to you

You can meditate for five minutes or forty. You can sit in silence, listen to sounds, walk, focus on your body, repeat a phrase, or use a spiritual practice. You can have thoughts the entire time and still be meditating. Learn the tools, experiment, keep what helps, and leave what does not.

You started. That matters.
